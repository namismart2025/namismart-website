When you're planning an office build-out, a camera system, or a new Wi-Fi network, someone eventually asks: "What cable are we running?" The usual answers are a shrug, "Cat6, I guess," or whatever the cheapest quote happened to include. That's a bigger decision than it looks, because cabling is the one part of a network you can't easily upgrade later. Routers, switches, and access points get swapped out every few years. The wire inside the walls usually stays for decades.

Here's what the differences between Cat5e, Cat6, and Cat6A actually mean in practice, and how to choose without overspending.

## The Short Version

| | Cat5e | Cat6 | Cat6A |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Typical speed** | 1 Gbps | 1 Gbps (10 Gbps up to ~55 m) | 10 Gbps full 100 m |
| **Max run** | 100 m / 328 ft | 100 m / 328 ft | 100 m / 328 ft |
| **Bandwidth** | 100 MHz | 250 MHz | 500 MHz |
| **Cable thickness** | Thinnest | Thicker | Thickest, stiffest |
| **Relative cost** | Lowest | Moderate | Highest |
| **Best for** | Existing runs already in place | Most homes and small offices | High-demand, long-lifespan, or noisy environments |

The numbers matter less than what they mean for your property. The sections below translate them.

## Cat5e: Fine Where It Already Is, Not Worth Pulling New

Cat5e has been the workhorse for two decades, and plenty of it is still doing its job in buildings across LA. If you already have properly terminated Cat5e running to desks and it works, there's usually no reason to rip it out.

For new installs, though, it rarely makes sense. The wire is cheap, but the labor of running it through attics, crawl spaces, and walls is the real cost. Pulling Cat5e today means paying that cost for a cable that has no headroom: it's capped at gigabit, and modern multi-gig access points and faster switches have nowhere to go. You'd be paying to open the same wall again in a few years.

## Cat6: The Right Default for Most Offices and Homes

Cat6 hits the sweet spot for most small businesses. It runs gigabit over the full 100 meters, has more headroom than Cat5e for 2.5G and 5G multi-gig links (now common on newer Wi-Fi access points), and it carries PoE for cameras, access points, and VoIP phones. It's what we spec for a typical office, retail space, or home unless there's a reason to go higher.

A few places where Cat6 is clearly the right call:

- **Standard office desk drops** — gigabit to the desk, with room to grow
- **PoE camera and access point runs** — reliable power and data over one cable (the same setup we cover in our post on [wired vs. wireless security cameras](/blog/wired-vs-wireless-security-cameras-la-homeowners))
- **Home networks** — a wired backbone to a few key rooms, with Wi-Fi covering the rest
- **Retrofit jobs** — where the cost of the run is mostly labor and Cat6 gives you a meaningful upgrade over what's there

## Cat6A: When It's Worth the Extra Cost

Cat6A doubles the bandwidth of Cat6 and holds 10-gigabit speeds across the entire 100-meter run. It's thicker, stiffer, and more expensive to buy and terminate, so it isn't a blanket upgrade. It earns its place in specific situations:

- **New construction or major remodels** where the walls are open and you want a 15–20 year build with no re-cabling
- **High-demand links** — offices with large file transfers, video editing, servers, or a lot of devices where 10G to key points is realistic soon
- **Long runs** where you need 10-gigabit and Cat6 would fall short of its roughly 55-meter limit
- **Large PoE bundles** — dozens of powered runs in the same pathway, where heat and interference add up
- **Noisy electrical environments** — runs alongside heavy electrical equipment, where Cat6A's better interference rejection matters

If none of that describes your space, Cat6 is usually the smarter spend.

## What About Cat7 and Cat8?

You'll see them advertised, and you almost never need them. Cat8 is built for short, very fast links inside data centers. Cat7 relies on connectors that aren't standard for typical Ethernet equipment. For a home, office, retail space, or small commercial building, Cat6 and Cat6A cover everything you'll realistically use.

## The Part Cable Category Doesn't Tell You

Picking the right grade is only half the job. A cheap or poorly installed run can undercut any category:

- **Copper-clad aluminum (CCA) cable** — sold as "Cat6" at suspiciously low prices, but it isn't pure copper. It performs worse, runs hotter under PoE, and can fail certification. Pure solid copper is what you want in walls.
- **Poor terminations** — a kinked cable, an untwisted pair at the jack, or a rushed punch-down can drop a Cat6A run to the performance of something much older
- **Over-long runs and tight bends** — past 100 meters, or bent sharper than the cable allows, performance degrades regardless of category
- **No testing or labeling** — a run that was never tested is a run you're guessing about, and an unlabeled one becomes a nightmare for the next technician

This is why we test every run and label both ends, and why the cable you choose should come from an installer who'll certify the result, not just pull wire and leave.

## FAQ

### Is Cat6 or Cat6A better for a small office?

For most small offices, Cat6 is the right default — it handles gigabit and multi-gig speeds over a full 100-meter run and carries PoE for cameras and access points. Cat6A is worth the extra cost where you want guaranteed 10-gigabit performance over longer runs, where cables bundle together near heavy electrical equipment, or where you're pulling wire in new construction and want a build that won't need re-cabling for 15–20 years.

### Should I still install Cat5e?

For new installs, generally no. The cable itself is cheap, but the labor to run it is the real cost, and Cat5e tops out at gigabit with no headroom for multi-gig access points or future speed upgrades. Existing Cat5e that's in good condition and terminated properly can keep working fine — it just isn't worth pulling new in a wall you'll have to open again later.

### Do I need Cat7 or Cat8 cable?

Almost never for a home or small business. Cat8 is designed for short, very high-speed runs inside data centers, and Cat7 uses connectors that aren't standard for typical Ethernet gear. Cat6 and Cat6A cover everything a small office, retail space, or home network will realistically need.

### Does the type of cable matter for PoE cameras and access points?

Yes, a little. Cat5e, Cat6, and Cat6A can all carry standard PoE, but better cable has lower resistance and handles heat better when many powered runs are bundled together — which matters more as camera and access point power draw climbs. It's one more reason we spec Cat6 as the minimum for PoE runs, and Cat6A for large or high-power bundles.

### What's the maximum length for an Ethernet cable run?

100 meters (about 328 feet) for the full channel, including patch cables at both ends, for Cat5e, Cat6, and Cat6A alike. Past that, you need a switch or media converter partway along the run. Cat6 can carry 10-gigabit over shorter runs (roughly 55 meters under good conditions), while Cat6A holds 10-gigabit across the full 100 meters.

### Is it worth paying for shielded cable?

Sometimes. Shielded (STP/FTP) cabling helps in environments with heavy electromagnetic interference — near large motors, high-voltage lines, or dense power bundles — but it must be grounded and terminated correctly or it can perform worse than unshielded. For a typical office or home, good unshielded Cat6 or Cat6A, installed and tested properly, is the right call.
